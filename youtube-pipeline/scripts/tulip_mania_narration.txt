Did you know a single tulip was once worth as much as a house? It happened in sixteen thirty seven.

Then, almost overnight, nobody would buy one.

To understand why, we need to go back to the Dutch Republic, which was booming, with ships trading all across the world.

Tulips had arrived from the Ottoman Empire, and rare striped varieties became the ultimate status symbol for the rich.

By the winter of sixteen thirty six, merchants and craftsmen were piling in.

They traded bulbs that were still in the ground, in taverns, using contracts instead of cash.

The Dutch called it the wind trade, because buyers were really trading in thin air.

At the peak of the craze, the most famous bulb of all, the Semper Augustus, reportedly sold for ten thousand guilders.

At the time, a skilled craftsman earned about three hundred guilders a year, so that one bulb was worth more than thirty years of wages.

In today's money, that's around a hundred and thirty thousand euros if you adjust for prices.

But if you measure it in wages, it's like paying over two million dollars for a single flower.

Then, in February sixteen thirty seven, buyers suddenly stopped showing up to the auctions.

Panic spread through the taverns, and prices collapsed.

In fact, many of those sky-high deals were probably never even paid.

But here's the real twist.

When historian Anne Goldgar searched the Dutch archives, she found only thirty seven people who had paid more than three hundred guilders for a bulb.

There was no wave of ruin, and no economic collapse.

So the world's most famous bubble might itself have been a bit of a bubble.
