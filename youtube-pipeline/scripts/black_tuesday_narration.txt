Did you know the American stock market once lost almost a quarter of its value in just two days?

It happened in October nineteen twenty nine.

It became known as the Wall Street Crash, and it was only the beginning.

To understand how it happened, we need to go back to the roaring twenties.

The economy was booming, and stock prices had been climbing for years.

Ordinary people wanted in, and many didn't even use their own money.

They bought on margin, putting down a small deposit, and borrowing the rest.

On September third, nineteen twenty nine, the Dow hit a record high of three hundred and eighty one.

Even the experts were convinced.

In mid October, Irving Fisher said stocks had reached what looks like a permanently high plateau.

Nine days later, the panic began.

On Black Thursday, October twenty fourth, prices fell so fast that the ticker tape fell far behind.

That afternoon, top bankers pooled their money and bought shares to calm the market. It worked, but only for a few days.

On Monday, the Dow fell almost thirteen percent.

On Tuesday, it fell another twelve. A record sixteen million shares changed hands on Black Tuesday alone.

The next day, the entertainment paper Variety ran one of the most famous headlines in history: Wall Street lays an egg.

Within weeks, around thirty billion dollars in share value had been wiped out. That's over five hundred billion dollars in today's money.

So why did it fall so fast? Prices had raced far ahead of real company profits.

When they started falling, lenders demanded their margin loans back.

Investors were forced to sell, which pushed prices even lower, and triggered even more margin calls.

And the fall kept going. By July nineteen thirty two, the Dow was down almost ninety percent from its peak.

A thousand dollars invested at the top was now worth around one hundred and eight dollars.

Thousands of banks failed, and America sank into the Great Depression.

It took twenty five years, until nineteen fifty four, for the Dow to get back to its old high.

The lesson? Borrowing to buy at the top can turn a crash into a catastrophe.
