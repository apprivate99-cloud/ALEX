# Next five videos — scripts

Same format as the current videos: a "did you know" hook, then the build-up, what happened, why,
the cost, a lesson, and a question end card. Each beat is one scene of roughly 3–6 seconds.
Numbers are written as they'll be spoken; captions convert them to digits.

Lines marked **⚠ check** rest on a single source or a commonly repeated figure; I'll confirm them
before rendering.

---

## 1. The Man Who Broke the Bank of England (Black Wednesday, 1992)

| # | Narration | Visual |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Did you know one man made around a billion dollars in a single day, by betting against the British pound? | Pound coin cracking in half; counter races to $1,000,000,000 |
| 2 | His name was George Soros, and the day became known as Black Wednesday. | Calendar flicks to 16 SEP 1992; "BLACK WEDNESDAY" slams in |
| 3 | In nineteen ninety, Britain had tied the pound to the German mark, inside a system called the ERM. | Map: Britain and Germany linked by a chain; "ERM" badge |
| 4 | The pound had to stay within a narrow range. If it fell too far, the Bank of England had to defend it. | A price band with a pound coin bouncing inside it |
| 5 | But by nineteen ninety two, Britain's economy was weak, and many traders thought the pound was overvalued. | Sad-faced Britain on the map; arrow pointing down |
| 6 | Soros agreed. His fund bet more than ten billion dollars that the pound would fall. | Soros character pushing a giant stack of chips onto "POUND ↓" |
| 7 | On the morning of September sixteenth, the selling began. The Bank of England bought pounds by the billion to hold the price up. | Bank building with money pouring out of the doors |
| 8 | Then it raised interest rates, from ten percent to twelve percent, in a single day. | Rate dial jumps 10% → 12% |
| 9 | It even announced a rise to fifteen percent. It didn't work. | Dial slams to 15%, then a "FAILED" stamp |
| 10 | That evening, Britain gave up and pulled the pound out of the ERM. | Chain on the map snaps; Britain's face goes shocked |
| 11 | The pound plunged. Soros reportedly walked away with around a billion dollars in profit. | Chart of the pound falling; counter on Soros's side |
| 12 | The Treasury later estimated the failed defence cost Britain around three point three billion pounds. | Bill / receipt "£3.3 BILLION" with "TAXPAYER" stamp |
| 13 | The twist? Many economists say leaving the ERM helped Britain, letting it cut interest rates and recover. | Rates dial falling; Britain's face recovers to happy |
| 14 | The lesson? Even a central bank can't hold a price the market doesn't believe in. | Lesson card |
| 15 | Would you have bet against the Bank of England? Follow for more finance history. | Question end card |

**Facts used:** date 16 Sep 1992; rates 10% → 12%, 15% announced; Soros shorted over $10bn and made about
$1bn; Treasury cost estimated at £3.3bn (2005 revision); UK left the ERM that evening.
**⚠ check:** the year Britain joined the ERM (1990), and "many economists say leaving helped".

## 2. The Island With Money Too Heavy to Move (Rai stones of Yap)

| # | Narration | Visual |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Did you know there's an island where money can be too heavy to move? | Huge stone disc on a beach; tiny person beside it |
| 2 | On Yap, in the Pacific, people used giant stone discs called rai as money. | Map zooms to Yap; a stone pops onto the island |
| 3 | The stones were carved from limestone on Palau, around four hundred kilometres away, and carried back by canoe. | Map: canoe route from Palau to Yap, with waves |
| 4 | The journey was dangerous, which is part of what made the stones valuable. | Storm, canoe rocking, a "RISK = VALUE" pill |
| 5 | But here's the clever part. The biggest stones were rarely moved at all. | Stone stays put; people walk past it |
| 6 | Instead, everyone simply remembered who owned each one. When a stone changed hands, the whole community knew. | Speech bubbles linking villagers to the stone; owner name changes |
| 7 | One story says a stone sank to the bottom of the sea on the way back from Palau. | Canoe tips; stone sinks with bubbles |
| 8 | Nobody ever saw it again, but everyone agreed it still existed, so its owner could still spend it. | Stone on the seabed with a glowing "STILL MONEY" tag |
| 9 | Money that works because everyone agrees on who owns what. Sound familiar? | Stone morphs into a bank-ledger / phone balance screen |
| 10 | That's basically how your bank balance works today: a shared record, not a pile of cash. | Ledger lines → phone balance |
| 11 | Rai stones are still exchanged on Yap for important occasions, like weddings and inheritances. | Ceremony scene with a stone and flowers |
| 12 | The lesson? Money doesn't need to move to be valuable. It just needs to be trusted. | Lesson card |
| 13 | Would you accept a stone you couldn't even see? Follow for more finance history. | Question end card |

**Facts used:** stones from Palau (~400 km); ownership tracked by oral record, not location; the sunken
stone still treated as valid; still used in traditional exchanges.
**⚠ check:** when quarrying began (sources give 500–600 years ago or later), and that limestone
is the right material.

## 3. Enron: The $70 Billion Lie

| # | Narration | Visual |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Did you know one of America's most admired companies went from a ninety dollar share price to less than a dollar, in about a year? | Share price counter tumbling 90 → 0.26; shocked face |
| 2 | Its name was Enron, an energy company from Houston, Texas. | Map pin on Houston; glass skyscraper with tilted "E" logo |
| 3 | Business magazines called it one of the most innovative companies in America. | Magazine covers stacking up with stars |
| 4 | Its share price soared to around ninety dollars in the year two thousand. | Line chart climbing |
| 5 | But behind the scenes, the profits weren't real. | Curtain pulls back on a messy back room |
| 6 | Enron booked profits it expected to make years in the future, as if it had already earned them. | Calendar pages flying backwards into a "PROFIT TODAY" box |
| 7 | And it hid billions in debt inside secret side companies, so it never showed up on its accounts. | Debt boxes being pushed into hidden doors labelled "SIDE COMPANY" |
| 8 | In late two thousand and one, the truth started to come out. | Newspaper headline flash; spotlight |
| 9 | Investors panicked, and the share price collapsed. | Chart crashes |
| 10 | On December second, two thousand and one, Enron filed for bankruptcy. | Calendar to 2 DEC 2001; "BANKRUPT" stamp |
| 11 | Thousands of employees lost their jobs, and many lost their retirement savings too, because their pensions were invested in Enron shares. | Office workers with cardboard boxes; savings jar draining |
| 12 | Shareholders lost an estimated seventy four billion dollars. | "$74 BILLION" counter |
| 13 | Its accountant, Arthur Andersen, was convicted of destroying evidence, and the firm collapsed too. | Shredder spitting paper; "GUILTY" stamp |
| 14 | The lesson? If a company's profits are too good to understand, ask why. | Lesson card |
| 15 | Would you have spotted the warning signs? Follow for more finance history. | Question end card |

**Facts used:** peak around $90 in mid-2000; under $1 by late 2001; Chapter 11 on 2 Dec 2001;
employees' 401(k)s heavily in Enron stock; shareholders lost about $74bn; Andersen convicted
June 2002 and lost its licence (the conviction was later overturned).
**⚠ check:** the "most innovative company" magazine accolade. Title: shareholder losses are about $74bn,
so "The $70 Billion Lie" is a rounded hook; "$74 Billion" is the strict version.

## 4. The Brothers Who Tried to Buy All the Silver (Silver Thursday, 1980)

| # | Narration | Visual |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Did you know two brothers once tried to buy so much silver that they could control its price? | Two cowboy-hat silhouettes on a mountain of silver bars |
| 2 | They were the Hunt brothers, billionaire oil heirs from Texas. | Texas map; oil derricks; two characters in 1970s suits |
| 3 | In the nineteen seventies, they started buying silver, lots of it, and kept buying. | Silver bars piling up in a vault |
| 4 | The price of silver rocketed, peaking at nearly fifty dollars an ounce in January nineteen eighty. | Chart soaring; "$48.70" peak marker |
| 5 | Much of their buying used borrowed money. | Bar split "YOURS / BORROWED" (reuse the margin graphic) |
| 6 | Regulators got worried. In January, the exchange brought in a new rule, Silver Rule seven, limiting how much silver could be bought on borrowed money. | Rulebook slams shut; "SILVER RULE 7" stamp |
| 7 | Silver prices started to slide, and the brothers' lenders wanted their money back. | Phone ringing; "MARGIN CALL" |
| 8 | On March twenty seventh, nineteen eighty, the Hunts missed a margin call of around a hundred million dollars. | Calendar to 27 MAR 1980; "MISSED" stamp |
| 9 | Silver crashed by half in a single day. It became known as Silver Thursday. | Chart halves; "SILVER THURSDAY" title |
| 10 | The panic was so bad that banks had to organise a huge rescue loan to stop the damage spreading. | Bank buildings linking arms around a cracked vault |
| 11 | The brothers eventually went bankrupt. | Silver mountain shrinking to one bar |
| 12 | The lesson? Trying to corner a market is a bet that you're bigger than everyone else, and you usually aren't. | Lesson card |
| 13 | Would you have bought silver at fifty dollars? Follow for more finance history. | Question end card |

**Facts used:** Silver Thursday was 27 Mar 1980; Silver Rule 7 adopted 7 Jan 1980; peak about $48.70;
a missed ~$100m margin call with Bache; prices halved that day.
**⚠ check:** the starting price in early 1979, the size of the bank rescue loan, and the bankruptcy
details (filed in 1988).

## 5. The 100 Trillion Dollar Banknote (Zimbabwe, 2008)

| # | Narration | Visual |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Did you know there's a banknote worth one hundred trillion dollars, that couldn't buy a bus ticket? | Giant "100 000 000 000 000" note; bus-ticket icon crossed out |
| 2 | It came from Zimbabwe, in two thousand and eight. | Map zooms to Zimbabwe; sad face |
| 3 | Zimbabwe's economy was shrinking, and the government paid its bills by printing more and more money. | Printing press spitting notes |
| 4 | Prices started rising faster and faster. | Price counter accelerating |
| 5 | By late two thousand and eight, prices were doubling roughly every day. | Calendar with a "×2" stamp on each day |
| 6 | Shops changed their prices several times a day, and people rushed to spend their money before it lost value. | Shopkeeper re-pricing; queue |
| 7 | The central bank kept adding zeros to its notes, until it printed the one hundred trillion dollar note. | Zeros flying onto the note one by one |
| 8 | By April two thousand and nine, Zimbabwe gave up on its own currency, and people switched to American dollars and South African rand. | Zimbabwe dollar note fading; $ and R coins arriving |
| 9 | Years later, the government offered to swap old notes for American dollars. The hundred trillion note was worth about forty cents. | Exchange counter: note → "$0.40" |
| 10 | Today, the notes sell to collectors for more than they were ever worth as money. | Note in a picture frame with a price tag |
| 11 | The lesson? When a government prints money to pay its bills, prices can spiral out of control. | Lesson card |
| 12 | Could it happen to your money? Follow for more finance history. | Question end card |

**Facts used:** prices doubling about every 24.7 hours (Hanke & Kwok); the Z$100 trillion note is dated 2008 and was
issued in January 2009; the currency was effectively abandoned in April 2009; the 2015 buy-back valued it at
about US$0.40.
**⚠ check:** "couldn't buy a bus ticket" (a popular claim; the note was worth roughly US$30 when issued). I'd
probably reword the hook to: "…worth one hundred trillion dollars, and a few years later it was worth forty cents."
