Did you know the first money wasn't coins at all? It was debt, scratched into clay over five thousand years ago.

That's not the story most of us are taught.

The usual version goes like this: before money, people bartered, swapping a cow for a few sacks of grain, until someone invented coins to make life easier.

There's just one problem. Anthropologists have found almost no evidence of any society that actually ran on barter.

So where did money really begin? The trail leads back to ancient Mesopotamia,

where temple scribes pressed records of debts into clay tablets. Before there were coins, there were IOUs.

Those debts were counted in barley, or in silver, weighed out in units called shekels.

Coins came much later.

About two thousand six hundred years ago, the kingdom of Lydia, in what's now Turkey, began stamping the world's first ones.

They were lumps of electrum, a natural mix of gold and silver, marked with a lion's head.

Then China changed everything.

Around a thousand years ago, merchants were tired of lugging heavy strings of iron coins, so they began trading paper receipts instead.

In ten twenty four, the government took over those receipts, and the world's first official paper money was born.

But it couldn't resist printing more and more of it, and over time, the notes became almost worthless. Sound familiar?

Today, most money isn't coins or paper at all. It's just numbers, stored in bank computers.

Which means that after five thousand years, money has come full circle: a record of who owes what, just like those clay tablets.
