## 1. The Man Who Gave the Ponzi Scheme Its Name

```
Did you know the Ponzi scheme is named after a real man, who made millions in just eight months?

His name was Charles Ponzi. In nineteen twenty, in Boston, he made people an incredible promise: a fifty percent return in just forty five days.

His trick sounded clever. International reply coupons were little postal vouchers that could be bought cheaply in Europe, and swapped for more valuable stamps in America.

Buy low, sell high. What could go wrong?

Money poured in. Ponzi reportedly took in around fifteen million dollars in just eight months.

But there was a problem. He wasn't really trading coupons at all.

He was paying early investors with money from new investors. As long as new money kept coming in, everyone looked rich.

Then a newspaper, the Boston Post, started asking questions.

It worked out that to cover all the money Ponzi had taken, he'd need around one hundred and sixty million coupons. Only about twenty seven thousand were in circulation.

The scheme collapsed. Ponzi was arrested, and later pleaded guilty to fraud.

Ponzi didn't invent the trick, but his name has been attached to it ever since.

The lesson? If someone promises huge returns with no risk, the money is probably coming from the next person in line.

Would you have invested with Ponzi? Follow for more finance history.
```

**Visuals:** a 1920s Boston street map; a "50% IN 45 DAYS" neon sign; postal coupons flying across the Atlantic; a money-pyramid animation where new investors' coins flow up to the earlier ones; the Boston Post front page; a "160,000,000 vs 27,000" comparison; handcuffs and a "GUILTY" stamp.
**Facts:** 50% in 45 days; about $15m in eight months (an estimate); the Boston Post exposé of July 1920, which worked out that 160 million coupons would be needed against about 27,000 in circulation; he pleaded guilty in late 1920.

## 2. The Trader Who Broke Britain's Oldest Merchant Bank

```
Did you know one young trader secretly lost so much money that he destroyed a bank more than two hundred years old?

His name was Nick Leeson, a star trader working for Barings Bank in Singapore.

Barings was one of the most respected banks in Britain. It had even served the royal family.

Leeson was meant to make safe, low risk trades. Instead, he made huge bets on the Japanese stock market.

When his bets lost, he hid the losses in a secret account, numbered eight eight eight eight eight, a lucky number in China.

Then he bet even bigger, to try to win it all back.

In January nineteen ninety five, a massive earthquake struck the Japanese city of Kobe, and the Japanese stock market fell sharply.

Leeson's bets collapsed. The hidden losses grew to around eight hundred and twenty seven million pounds.

Leeson fled, leaving a note that simply said, I'm sorry.

On February twenty sixth, nineteen ninety five, Barings collapsed. It was later sold to a Dutch bank, for just one pound.

Leeson was caught, and sentenced to six and a half years in a Singapore prison.

The lesson? When nobody checks the numbers, one person can bring down an entire bank.

Would you have spotted the secret account? Follow for more finance history.
```

**Visuals:** a Singapore skyline at night; a trading floor; a ledger with a hidden "88888" account glowing; a map of Japan with a sad face as the Kobe earthquake shakes it; a losses counter running to £827m; a note reading "I'm sorry"; a "SOLD FOR £1" stamp on the bank building; prison bars.
**Facts:** losses hidden in account 88888; the Kobe earthquake on 17 January 1995; losses of about £827m (sources range from £827m to £860m); collapse on 26 February 1995; sold to ING for £1; sentenced to six and a half years.
**⚠ check before rendering:** the "I'm sorry" note and the royal-family link. Both are widely reported, but I'll confirm them, or drop them if I can't.

## 3. The Most Expensive Pizza in History

```
Did you know someone once paid for two pizzas with bitcoin that would later be worth more than a billion dollars?

On May twenty second, twenty ten, a programmer named Laszlo Hanyecz posted an offer online.

He'd pay ten thousand bitcoins to anyone who would get him two pizzas.

Back then, bitcoin was a strange new experiment. Hardly anyone used it to buy real things.

A man in Britain took the deal. He ordered two Papa John's pizzas to Laszlo's house in Florida, and received ten thousand bitcoins in return.

At the time, those coins were worth about forty one dollars.

It was the first famous time bitcoin was used to buy something in the real world.

Then bitcoin took off.

Those same ten thousand coins became worth thousands of dollars, then millions, and at bitcoin's record highs, more than a billion dollars.

Laszlo says he doesn't regret it. Someone had to prove that bitcoin could actually be spent.

Today, May twenty second is celebrated as Bitcoin Pizza Day.

The lesson? Nobody knows what money will be worth in the future, not even the people using it.

Would you have kept the bitcoin, or eaten the pizza? Follow for more finance history.
```

**Visuals:** a 2010 internet forum post; a pizza box with a bitcoin logo; a map with the order going from Britain to Florida; a "$41" price tag; a counter rocketing from $41 to over $1,000,000,000; two pizza slices glowing gold; a "BITCOIN PIZZA DAY" calendar card.
**Facts:** 22 May 2010; 10,000 BTC for two Papa John's pizzas; worth about $41 at the time (sources give $25–41); worth about $1.1bn at bitcoin's record highs.
**Note:** keep "more than a billion dollars at its record highs" rather than a fixed figure, because bitcoin's price keeps changing.

## 4. The Con Man Who Sold a Country That Didn't Exist

```
Did you know a Scottish con man once convinced people to invest in a country that didn't exist?

His name was Gregor MacGregor. In the eighteen twenties, he arrived in London claiming to be the ruler of a land called Poyais, in Central America.

He described a paradise: fertile soil, rivers full of gold, and a beautiful capital city waiting for settlers.

He even printed Poyais banknotes, and sold land to eager investors.

London's investors loved it. MacGregor raised a loan of around two hundred thousand pounds for his new nation.

Then he sent the first settlers off to start their new lives.

When they arrived, there was no capital city. No gold. No country at all. Just untouched jungle, on the coast of what's now Honduras.

The settlers were stranded. Disease spread quickly, and more than half of them died.

MacGregor slipped away to France, and tried the same trick all over again.

Incredibly, he never spent long in prison, and kept running versions of the scheme for years.

The lesson? Always check that what you're investing in actually exists.

Would you have bought land in Poyais? Follow for more finance history.
```

**Visuals:** a grand portrait-style MacGregor in a military uniform; a glossy fake "Poyais" brochure with a golden city; Poyais banknotes; a London bond office with a "£200,000" counter; ships sailing to Central America on a map; arrival at an empty jungle where the brochure city fades away; a sad-faced map; MacGregor sneaking off to France.
**Facts:** the London loan of about £200,000; settlers sailed in 1822–23; Poyais was on the Honduras coast; more than half died (estimates range from about half to two-thirds); he later tried a similar scheme in France.

## 5. When the Geniuses Lost Billions

```
Did you know a hedge fund run by two Nobel Prize winners lost four point six billion dollars in less than four months?

It was called Long Term Capital Management, and it was founded in nineteen ninety four by some of the smartest people on Wall Street.

Its partners included Myron Scholes and Robert Merton, who won the Nobel Prize in economics in nineteen ninety seven.

Their computer models found tiny price differences in markets, and bet on them disappearing.

The profits on each trade were small, so they borrowed enormous amounts of money to make them bigger.

For a few years, it worked brilliantly. Investors earned huge returns.

Then, in August nineteen ninety eight, Russia defaulted on its debt.

Panic spread through the markets, and the prices their models expected never came.

Because the fund had borrowed so much, the small losses quickly became gigantic.

In less than four months, it lost four point six billion dollars.

The fund was so big that officials feared its collapse could damage the entire financial system.

So the Federal Reserve Bank of New York brought together fourteen banks, which put in around three point six billion dollars to rescue it.

The lesson? Even geniuses can't predict everything, and borrowed money makes every mistake bigger.

Would you have trusted the geniuses? Follow for more finance history.
```

**Visuals:** a Nobel medal spinning; Wall Street towers; computer screens full of formulas; a "BORROWED" bar dwarfing a small "YOURS" bar (the margin graphic again); a map of Russia with a shocked face and a "DEFAULT" stamp; a losses counter running to $4.6bn; fourteen bank buildings linking arms around the fund.
**Facts:** founded 1994; Scholes and Merton won the Nobel in 1997; Russia defaulted in August 1998; $4.6bn lost in under four months; the New York Fed brokered a roughly $3.6bn rescue by 14 banks in September 1998.
**Note:** the fund's name is spelled out because its abbreviation would be misread by the voice.

## 6. When Reddit Took On Wall Street

```
Did you know a group of ordinary people on the internet once sent a struggling video game shop's shares up more than twenty times in a single month?

In January twenty twenty one, GameStop was a struggling video game retailer, and its shares were worth around seventeen dollars.

Big hedge funds were betting that GameStop would fail. They'd shorted more of its shares than were actually available to trade.

But users on a Reddit forum called WallStreetBets spotted something.

If enough people bought the shares, the hedge funds would be forced to buy them back too, sending the price even higher. It's called a short squeeze.

So they started buying. Lots of them.

The price exploded. Within days, GameStop shares hit highs of nearly five hundred dollars.

One hedge fund, Melvin Capital, was hit so hard it needed billions of dollars in emergency funding.

Then, on January twenty eighth, some trading apps, including Robinhood, suddenly stopped people from buying the shares.

The price crashed back down, and many people who'd bought near the top lost money.

The story ended up in front of the United States Congress.

The lesson? Markets can be moved by crowds, but crowds can crash just as fast.

Would you have bought GameStop? Follow for more finance history.
```

**Visuals:** a GameStop-style store front (generic, no logo); a "$17" price tag; a hedge fund tower with "SHORT" arrows; a Reddit-style forum post with rockets; the short squeeze explained with arrows forcing buying; a price chart rocketing to nearly $500; the Melvin tower cracking; a phone trading app with a "BUY DISABLED" button; the chart crashing; the Capitol building.
**Facts:** $17.25 at the start of January 2021; about 140% of the float shorted; a high of about $483 at close on 28 January, with intraday above $460 and pre-market over $500, so "nearly five hundred dollars" is safe; Melvin received a $2.75bn injection from Citadel and Point72; Robinhood restricted buying on 28 January; there were Congressional hearings in February 2021.
**Note:** use a generic shop design rather than the real GameStop logo, to avoid copyright issues.
